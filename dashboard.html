Germany's cross-border power balance

The price of German power swings several-fold within a day, and cross-border flows follow it: in when it is dear, out when it is cheap. The cheap hours are cheap because wind and solar are running.

The gap between buying and selling
—

Three facts that stop the numbers above being read as a verdict. Every seller received at least what it asked and every buyer paid at most what it offered, so nothing here was bought or sold unwillingly — that is what a uniform clearing price guarantees: everyone who clears is paid the same market price, not their own bid, so most sellers receive more than they asked for. (Block orders are the small exception; they can be accepted against their own limit.) The cheap hours are cheap because wind and solar are running; without them those hours would price like the expensive ones, and German consumers would pay more, not less. And importing in the dear hours lowers the German price rather than raising it, because the imported megawatt-hour displaces a more expensive German plant. What is left is a real fact with real consequences: the value of a megawatt-hour in Germany varies several-fold within the day.

the negative hour control hours

The shape is the argument. Weather is smooth in time, so a badly-forecast windy spell cannot produce a shortfall one hour wide — it would slope in and out across the neighbouring hours. A shutdown triggered by the hour's own price can, and this one does: the deficit collapses by more than an order of magnitude in the very next fifteen-minute block, and every control block scatters around zero in both directions. Fifteen minutes is the market's own settlement interval, not a window chosen to flatter the result.

What this cannot tell you is who stopped. Below zero a plant selling into the market loses money on every megawatt-hour, so feathering the blades is the owner's own call — but only for plants whose support premium is actually withdrawn, which under § 51 EEG depends on commissioning date: never for plants older than 2016, after six, four or one consecutive negative hours for later vintages, and from the first negative quarter-hour only for plants commissioned since February 2025. Curtailment ordered by the grid operator, because northern wind cannot reach southern demand, lands in the same high-wind hours and looks identical here. Price-conditioning cannot separate the two; the ordered part is counted in the redispatch spending under both sides of the ledger.

The gap the headline leads with is largely a summer phenomenon; Germany changes from net exporter to net importer partway through the spring; and solar's discount to the market average, the thing that makes cheap afternoons cheap, essentially does not exist in January, when there is little solar to depress its own price.

Where does this money come from, and where does it go?

The rent, in and out

In: when a border is full, the two sides clear at different prices. Buyers in the dearer zone pay their own zonal price and sellers in the cheaper zone receive theirs; the gap on each megawatt-hour that crossed is collected by the coupling mechanism and paid to the two transmission operators on that border, normally split evenly. Nobody is billed for it separately — it is the wedge between what the dearer zone paid and what the cheaper zone received, and both sides bear part of it.

One border here is not like the others. Switzerland sits outside the coupled day-ahead market, so its capacity is auctioned explicitly instead and no coupling mechanism collects the spread. The Swiss figure in the chart above is still price difference times flow, but what the operators actually earn there is auction revenue, generally a good deal less. It is about an eighth of the rent shown, so read the total as the coupled borders plus a Swiss approximation.

Out: the operators cannot keep it. Article 19 of Regulation (EU) 2019/943 puts it to two priority uses first — guaranteeing that the capacity they sold stays firm, including compensation when it does not, and maintaining or increasing cross-zonal capacity, whether by remedial actions or by building interconnection. Only once those are satisfied may it be counted as income when network tariffs are set, and anything left has to sit in a separate account until it can be spent on those purposes.

The spending, in and out

Out: the grid inside Germany constrains flows the market ignored, so the operator intervenes. In redispatch it orders one power station down and another up on the far side of the bottleneck, and compensates both for the change. The same budget covers stations held in reserve outside the market for exactly this, contracted load reductions, and countertrading — buying and selling across a border to relieve a constraint.

In: from every electricity customer. It sits in the transmission operators' cost base and is recovered through their network charges — since 2023 a single nationwide transmission tariff, equalised between the four operators rather than charged by region — then rolled into distribution tariffs and on to bills. Not evenly, though: energy-intensive and atypical consumers can have their network fees cut sharply under § 19(2) StromNEV, and the shortfall that creates is refinanced by a separate surcharge on everyone else. Those same reliefs are why the industrial bar in the panel below sits so far under the household one.

So both ends of this chart reach the same place: the grid fee on a bill. Rent can only lower it after the capacity obligations are met; redispatch raises it. And that fee sits in the roughly three-fifths of a household bill in the panel below that is levies, network charges and tax — mostly fixed, though the VAT inside it rides on the whole bill and so does move with the wholesale price.
