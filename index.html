Germany's cross-border power balance

The price of German power swings several-fold within a day, and cross-border flows follow it: in when it is dear, out when it is cheap. The cheap hours are cheap because wind and solar are running.

The gap between buying and selling
—

Three facts that stop the numbers above being read as a verdict. Every trade cleared at a price its seller had bid, so nothing here was sold below cost and nothing was bought unwillingly — a uniform-price auction cannot do either. The cheap hours are cheap because wind and solar are running; without them those hours would price like the expensive ones, and German consumers would pay more, not less. And importing in the dear hours lowers the German price rather than raising it, because the imported megawatt-hour displaces a more expensive German plant. What is left is a real fact with real consequences: the value of a megawatt-hour in Germany varies several-fold within the day.

Where does this money come from, and where does it go?

The rent, in and out

In: when a border is full, the two sides clear at different prices. Buyers in the dearer zone pay their own zonal price and sellers in the cheaper zone receive theirs; the gap on each megawatt-hour that crossed is collected by the coupling mechanism and paid to the two transmission operators on that border, normally split evenly. Nobody is billed for it separately — it is already inside the price the dearer zone paid.

Out: the operators cannot keep it. Article 19 of Regulation (EU) 2019/943 puts it to two priority uses first — guaranteeing that the capacity they sold stays firm, including compensation when it does not, and maintaining or increasing cross-zonal capacity, whether by remedial actions or by building interconnection. Only once those are satisfied may it be counted as income when network tariffs are set, and anything left has to sit in a separate account until it can be spent on those purposes.

The spending, in and out

Out: the grid inside Germany constrains flows the market ignored, so the operator intervenes. In redispatch it orders one power station down and another up on the far side of the bottleneck, and compensates both for the change. The same budget covers stations held in reserve outside the market for exactly this, loads paid to be interruptible, and countertrading — buying and selling across a border to relieve a constraint.

In: from every electricity customer. The operators recover it through network charges, passed to the distribution operators and on to consumers, spread across all German users by a mechanism descended from the § 19 StromNEV levy. Domestic and industrial alike, in proportion to network use.

So both ends of this chart reach the same place: the grid fee on a bill. Rent can only lower it after the capacity obligations are met; redispatch raises it. And that fee is part of the fixed two-thirds of a household bill in the panel below — the part that does not move when the wholesale price does.
